\section{System Context and Fairness Scope}

The artifact under analysis is a threat-aware quantum-routing controller with
fairness-evaluation and mediation components. At each decision round, it
observes a possibly incomplete network state, uses a bandit policy to select an
entanglement route, and allocates limited quantum capacity under stochastic
links, partial feedback, and configurable threats. Link quality, memory
constraints, and attacks affect route quality and realized performance;
allocator choice shapes qubit distribution, while replay capacity controls how
much experience replay-based policies retain for learning.
These decisions are consequential because entanglement distribution depends on
probabilistic link success, scarce resources, and competition among
requests~\cite{wehner2018quantum,pant2019routing}. Existing components support
post-run group evaluation, and a quantum-specific mediation contract exists for
future live integration.

Fairness is allocative: strong network-wide efficiency can coexist with
systematically worse service for some flows. The controller affects whether
requests succeed, their latency and retry burden, and whether repeated
allocations cause starvation. Stakeholders include competing applications or
tenants, operators responsible for allocation policy, and downstream users of
the resulting entanglement paths. Prespecified equity units may include
latency-sensitive versus delay-tolerant traffic, high- versus low-demand
requests, or high- versus low-context-quality cohorts. These are proposed
operational groups, not demographic or validated cohorts, and must not be
defined after observing outcomes. If service classes become associated with
people or institutions, protected-attribute analysis and stakeholder review
would also be necessary. Quantum-routing research treats finite capacity and
simultaneous requests as fair-allocation problems, supporting analysis beyond
aggregate throughput~\cite{li2021effective,cicconetti2023service}.

\section{Fairness Risk Analysis}

Bias can enter throughout the routing pipeline. Following established
life-cycle taxonomies~\cite{suresh2021framework,mehrabi2021survey},
representation harm arises when workloads omit difficult topologies,
low-context flows, or relevant service classes. Measurement harm arises when
the quantum trace lacks demand, allocation, route, fidelity, retry, starvation,
or context-quality evidence needed to interpret group-conditioned outcomes.
Aggregation harm occurs when one reward function treats different latency,
fidelity, or demand requirements as interchangeable. Evaluation harm occurs
when aggregate throughput or reward conceals class-level outcomes, and
deployment harm arises when a policy is transferred across materially different
topologies, demand distributions, or threat regimes.

Sequential learning can compound these disparities because a bandit observes
rewards only for selected actions. Unequal selection produces unequal evidence;
depending on exploration, reward estimates, context quality, and threats, that
imbalance can influence later decisions. Fair-bandit research shows that
fairness constraints alter exploration and regret rather than acting as
cost-free post-processing~\cite{joseph2016fairness}; contextual-allocation work
likewise motivates explicit minimum-service concerns~\cite{chen2020fair}.
Incomplete or stale context can therefore disadvantage a flow without any
protected attribute, while nonstationarity may concentrate failures, retries,
or delay on one class despite acceptable aggregate performance.

Plausible harms include longer waits for latency-sensitive traffic,
demand-normalized under-service of high-demand flows, and poorer decisions for
weak-context flows. Long or low-fidelity routes may be repeatedly
deprioritized until requests starve. These are allocative harms because access
to a scarce capability is degraded, reliability harms when failures or delay
persist, and accountability harms when operators cannot reconstruct why a flow
received inferior service.

\section{Fairness Objectives, Mitigations, and Validation}

Classical criteria are useful reference points but do not transfer mechanically
to quantum-resource allocation. Demographic parity supplies an analogous test
of access or success rates across groups; equalized odds and equality of
opportunity concern prediction errors conditional on outcomes
~\cite{hardt2016equality}. Calibration---agreement between predicted and
observed success---and parity may conflict under different base rates, and
desirable criteria cannot generally be satisfied together
~\cite{chouldechova2017fair,kleinberg2017tradeoffs}. Equal raw allocation may
also conflict with equal success when demand and path quality differ. The
primary objective is therefore demand-normalized service equity, supplemented
by group outcomes and a starvation constraint.

For group $g$ over horizon $T$, define
\begin{equation}
 A_g(T)=\frac{\sum_{t=1}^{T}Q_{g,t}}{\sum_{t=1}^{T}D_{g,t}},
 \qquad
 \Delta_A(T)=\max_{g,h}|A_g(T)-A_h(T)|,
\end{equation}
where $Q_{g,t}$ is allocated capacity and $D_{g,t}$ is declared demand. Audits
should also report success and latency gaps, Jain's index over normalized
access, worst-group outcomes, retries, starvation, and supported fidelity
failures~\cite{jain1984quantitative}. Experiments must prespecify group
definitions, denominators, zero-demand exclusions, missing-context rules, and
physical-infeasibility conditions.

The first mitigation is an enriched decision trace containing group, demand,
context quality, candidate and selected routes, policy scores, allocated
capacity, fidelity, success, latency, retries, and starvation age. A bounded,
auditable intervention at the policy-score or allocator boundary could use
\begin{equation}
 S_t^{\mathrm{fair}}(a)=U_t(a)
 -\lambda[\widehat{\Delta}_t(a)-\epsilon]_+-\gamma C_t(a),
\end{equation}
where $U_t(a)$ is utility, $\widehat{\Delta}_t(a)$ estimated disparity,
$\epsilon$ tolerated slack, and $C_t(a)$ a missing-context penalty. Under this
proposed formulation and without a hard constraint, $\lambda=\gamma=0$ recovers
the base score and defines the unmediated comparison condition; a feasible
service floor or starvation limit may supplement the penalty~\cite{chen2020fair}.

Validation should compare mediated and unmediated configurations across matched
policies, allocators, replay-capacity settings, topologies, threats, and random
seeds. Reports should place utility, regret, success, and fidelity beside equity measures,
worst-group outcomes, and uncertainty intervals. The prespecified
utility--equity frontier, rather than a favorable post hoc operating point,
should guide selection. Fidelity and safety constraints remain binding even
when disparity penalties or service floors reduce throughput, increase other
flows' latency, or spend resources on physically disadvantaged routes.

\section{Broader Impacts}

The broader aim is to prevent aggregate performance from becoming the sole
objective when scarce quantum-network resources are allocated across competing
flows. A service-equity layer can expose disparities and make intervention
costs auditable. The claim is deliberately narrow: the project should measure
flow-conditioned outcomes and evaluate a mediator and its utility--equity
frontier without establishing that a network or allocation policy is socially
fair.

\section*{Limitations}

Live fairness-mediated quantum evaluation remains prospective. Fairness
conclusions from simulation will be limited by modeled workloads, topologies,
physical assumptions, and threats. Service classes are engineering choices, demand
normalization embeds assumptions, criteria can conflict, and small classes can
yield unstable estimates. Repeated trials and uncertainty intervals are
therefore necessary; insufficient evidence must be reported and not treated
as absence of disparity, and any service floor must be justified and not assumed fair. Mediation may optimize an imperfect proxy or reduce utility.
Deployment would require stakeholder review, hardware-grounded validation,
transparent class and denominator rules, authority over alerts and weights, a
rollback procedure, and continuing audits. Manipulation of threat or routing
signals and exposure of flow telemetry require separate security and privacy
analysis.
